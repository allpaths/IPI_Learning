Where this section fits#

The Conclusions restate the prediction and the main results, list what is needed to build the experiment, and name three next steps that would test the weakest assumptions before anyone asks for beam time.

Paragraph by paragraph#

Paragraph 1: what was found#

Paper · VI, paragraph 1… predicts that each electron–positron annihilation at temperature \(T\) releases two photons with \(\lambda T = 13.76\) mm K for 1.509 bits per particle. … The signature is a line that moves from 179 µm to 92 µm when the annihilation site is warmed from 77 K to 150 K. Its main limitation is the film itself, which lets out only about 7% of the photons and whose thermal emission sets the noise. Under the stated assumptions, a 5σ detection would take tens of minutes to hours at an existing slow-positron facility.

One prediction, one signature, one limitation, one feasibility statement. Note "under the stated assumptions": the conclusion doesn't outrun the estimates.

Paragraph 2: what you need#

Paper · VI, paragraph 2Building the experiment requires a slow-positron beam of at least \(10^7\) positrons per second at about 100 eV; a nanometer-thin aluminum film on a high-resistivity silicon substrate with calibrated temperature control at 77–150 K; a collecting cavity at 4–10 K; cold band-pass filters …; a far-infrared bolometer; and a gamma-ray detector shell …

A shopping list. The 10⁷ threshold comes from Table II: at 10⁷ s⁻¹ the 77 K run takes about a day (1.1 d; it was 10 hours before the positronium fraction was corrected from 15% to 48%), still a feasible beam-time block. The cell below inverts Eq. (11) to show what rate gives a detection in 1 or 8 hours.

Paragraph 3: next steps#

Paper · VI, paragraph 3Three steps would test the main assumptions before beam time is requested. The first is a bench measurement of the far-infrared emissivity of the actual film on its substrate … The second is a measurement of the back-scattered and positronium fractions for 100 eV positrons on the same film, at 77 K and 150 K and after controlled oxygen doses … Next to \(f_\mathrm{esc}\), the positronium fraction is the largest uncertainty: published values for aluminum range from near zero for a thick oxide to about 0.9. The third is contact with a slow-positron facility …

Ordered from cheapest to hardest. Measuring emissivity needs no positrons at all, just a far-IR spectrometer and a cryostat, and directly checks \(f_\mathrm{esc}\) (by Kirchhoff, emissivity = escape). The positron measurements check \(f_\mathrm{imp}\) and \(f_\mathrm{film}\); after reading Mills's papers, \(f_\mathrm{film}\) is the one most worth checking, because it depends on what is on the film's surface. The facility conversation checks whether the cavity and bolometer can be fitted to a real beamline.

Worked numbers#

# scripts/explainer_build/cells/c07_rate.py # What beam rate gives a 5-sigma detection in a given time? (Sec. VI) import sys, os; sys.path.insert(0, "../../simulation") import sensitivity as S for T in (77.0, 150.0): b = S.budget(T) for hours in (1, 8): # tau = (s^2/2) NEP^2/(kappa R p)^2 -> R = s NEP / (kappa p sqrt(2 tau)) R = S.SIGMA * b["NEP"] / (S.KAPPA * b["Psig_per_R"] * (2 * hours * 3600) ** 0.5) print(f"T = {T:.0f} K: 5 sigma in {hours} h needs R = {R:.1e} positrons/s")
T = 77 K: 5 sigma in 1 h needs R = 5.2e+07 positrons/s T = 77 K: 5 sigma in 8 h needs R = 1.9e+07 positrons/s T = 150 K: 5 sigma in 1 h needs R = 1.4e+08 positrons/s T = 150 K: 5 sigma in 8 h needs R = 5.0e+07 positrons/s

So "at least 10⁷" is roughly the rate where a day or so of beam suffices at 77 K (about 2×10⁷ for 8 hours); about 5×10⁷ gives an hour. These assume the clean-surface positronium fraction of 0.48; a film with more positronium would need more beam.

Questions a referee might ask#

Q1. You've designed an experiment but not done it. What is the publishable result?

Honest answer: a quantitative, falsifiable design: the observable, its signature, the dominant noise, and the resources needed, with every input traceable to a script. AIP Advances publishes such studies; the value is that someone with a beamline can decide whether it's worth doing.

Q2. Have you contacted a facility?

Honest answer: not yet; the paper lists it as a next step. It would be better to have an informal feasibility comment from beamline scientists before submission, if possible.

Key takeaways#

  • Signature: a line that jumps from 179 µm to 92 µm between 77 K and 150 K.
  • Needs ≥10⁷ slow positrons/s, a 5 nm Al film on silicon, a 4–10 K cavity, cold filters, a bolometer, a gamma shell.
  • Next: measure emissivity, then positron fractions, then talk to a facility.
