Where this section fits#

Section V answers "what would the apparatus record?" It is the quantitative core: what is computed and what is assumed (V.A), what the signal and background look like (V.B), what is lost in the film (V.C), how noisy it is and how long a detection takes (V.D), what could fake it (V.E), and what a result would mean (V.F).

Paragraph by paragraph#

A. How the estimates are made#

Paper · V.AThe estimates in this section come from four short Python scripts … The Geant4 model … is used only for quantities that depend on the geometry and on the transport of positrons and gamma rays … Geant4 contains no physics for the predicted photons or for the soft-photon background in the far infrared. Both enter the model as inputs … so the model cannot confirm either of them.

This paragraph fixes an important logical point. A simulation can only output what its physics contains. Geant4 knows nothing about meV photons, so the signal and background spectra were typed in. When the simulation then "shows" a large signal-to-background ratio, it is showing back the inputs, multiplied by the collection efficiency. The paper therefore never says "Geant4 confirms" the separation.

The last paragraph is the AI-use disclosure that AIP's policy asks for when AI tools contribute to analysis or code. It states that AI tools helped draft and edit the text, write and run the analysis scripts, and search for and check references, and that the author takes responsibility for the content.

B. Signal and background spectra#

Paper · V.B, paragraphs 1–2Every annihilation also produces a few low-energy photons through ordinary quantum electrodynamics (QED). Charges that are created, destroyed or suddenly deflected radiate soft photons, whose number per unit logarithmic energy interval is nearly constant at low energy … \( \frac{dN}{d\ln E} \approx \frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\langle|\boldsymbol\beta_+-\boldsymbol\beta_-|^2\rangle \) …

Whenever charges appear, vanish or jolt, they shake the electromagnetic field and emit a spray of soft photons, with roughly equal numbers per factor-of-\(e\) in energy. How many depends on how fast the charges were moving. In a metal, the positron has slowed to thermal speed, but the electron it meets moves at the Fermi velocity, about 0.7% of light speed. Plugging that in (Eq. 6) gives 4×10⁻⁹ photons per annihilation in a 10% band. As a deliberately pessimistic bound, the paper also uses the formula for fast charges, which gives 1.7×10⁻⁴. Either way the predicted two photons per annihilation are at least 10⁴ times more.

Paper · V.B, paragraph 3The background is also nearly independent of the film temperature, because the electron velocities that set it are fixed by the Fermi energy. … The background therefore stays at the same wavelengths when the film temperature changes, while the predicted line moves.

The "thermal share" in the output above (0.14% at 77 K, 0.28% at 150 K) is the part of \(\langle\beta^2\rangle\) that depends on \(T\). It is tiny, so the background is flat in \(T\).

C. Losses in the target#

Paper · V.C, escapeThe largest is escape from the film. … By Kirchhoff's law … the probability that a photon generated inside the film escapes is equal to the film's absorptivity for light arriving from outside … \(A(d) = 2g/(1+g)^2,\ g = Z_0\sigma d/2\) … The full calculation … gives \(f_\mathrm{esc} = 0.067\) for the 5 nm film …

Kirchhoff's law says a surface emits exactly as well as it absorbs. So instead of tracking photons trying to get out of a film (hard), compute how much light shone on the film it absorbs (a textbook transfer-matrix calculation). For a thin metal film the answer is the simple Eq. (7): \(g\) is the film's sheet conductance measured in units of free space's impedance. Absorption peaks at 50% when \(g=1\), which for aluminum is at 0.17 nm, thinner than an atom. Any real film has \(g\gg1\), and absorption (so escape) falls as \(2/g\propto1/d\). At 5 nm, 6.75%.

Two cautions from the paper: the Kirchhoff identification is an approximation, and the bulk room-temperature Drude parameters probably underestimate escape for a 5 nm film (thin films are more resistive). Hence the bench emissivity test in Section VI.

Paper · V.C, back-scattering and positroniumWe assume that 10% are reflected at 100 eV … Because the detection time scales as \(f_\mathrm{imp}^{-2}\), a reflected fraction of 30% instead of 10% would lengthen it by a factor of 1.65. … Our model assumes a temperature-independent fraction of 15% below that range, giving an in-film fraction \(f_\mathrm{film} = 0.85\) …

Two smaller losses. Some positrons bounce off (10%, assumed; measured values exist only at keV energies). Some reach the surface and leave as positronium (15%, assumed, rising sharply above 200 K). Both are things to measure, not to argue about.

D. Noise and time to a detection#

Paper · V.D, Eqs. (8)–(11)\(P_\mathrm{sig} = 2R\,\mathrm{Br}\,f_\mathrm{imp}f_\mathrm{film}f_\mathrm{esc}f_\mathrm{coll}\,\eta\,E_I\) … The noise comes from the film itself … \(n(E_I) = 1/(e^{E_I/k_bT}-1) = 1/(2^I-1) = 0.54\) … \(\mathrm{NEP}^2 = 2h\nu P_\mathrm{th}[1+\eta f_\mathrm{coll}f_\mathrm{esc}n(E_I)] + \mathrm{NEP}_\mathrm{det}^2\) … \(\tau = \frac{s^2}{2}\frac{\mathrm{NEP}^2}{(\kappa P_\mathrm{sig})^2}\) …

Read Eq. (8) as a chain of survival fractions: two photons per annihilation, times the fraction that implants, annihilates in the film, escapes, is collected, and is detected. At 150 K the chain multiplies to 0.025. Eq. (9) is the key physics: because the photon energy is tied to \(k_bT\), the line always sits at the same point on the film's thermal spectrum. Eq. (10) is the standard photon-noise formula for a detector looking at a warm source. Eq. (11) turns noise and signal into an integration time.

Paper · V.D, scalingThe time therefore grows as \(T^3\) … Cooling does not separate the signal from the thermal spectrum, since the signal always sits at the same point on it, but it reduces the noise faster than the signal. A colder measurement is faster.

This resolves what looks like a paradox. Cooling doesn't help spectrally (the line can't escape the thermal hump), but it helps absolutely: noise power falls as \(T^4\), noise amplitude as \(T^{5/2}\), signal only as \(T\). So time ∝ \(T^5/T^2 = T^3\), and 77 K is 7.4× faster than 150 K.

E. Systematic effects#

Paper · V.EPositronium formation changes how many annihilations happen in the film … but not the wavelength … It is an open question at what wavelength, if any, positronium annihilating in vacuum would radiate … Beam heating gives a chopped change in the film's thermal emission. It is broadband, so it would appear in a control band …

Each effect either changes intensity without moving the line (positronium), sits at the wrong wavelength (back-scattering to a cold wall), or is broadband (heating). None mimics a narrow line that jumps between bands with \(T\).

F. What a result would mean#

Paper · V.FA positive result would be a chopped signal, proportional to the gamma-ray count, in the band at \(\lambda_1\) at \(T_1\) and in the band at \(\lambda_2\) at \(T_2\), absent from the control band … The product \(\lambda T\) would then measure the information content directly … A null result would set an upper limit on Br … It would bear on the literal-energy reading of M/E/I together with assumptions 1–5, not on M/E/I as a whole.

Positive: four conditions at once, and then \(I = hc/(k_b\lambda T\ln2)\) is a measurement. Null: a limit on the emitting fraction Br, which improves as √time; silent on the gamma-imprint alternative (a 3×10⁻⁸ energy shift, far below gamma spectroscopy) and on very different \(I\).

Questions a referee might ask#

Q1. Your signal-to-background ratio comes from a simulation in which you typed in both spectra. Isn't that circular?

Honest answer: it would be if presented as a result; the paper doesn't. The background is estimated analytically (Eq. 6 and an upper bound), and both spectra are described as model inputs. The simulation only supplies collection geometry.

Q2. Why use the slow-charge formula when the standard radiative-correction coefficient is 1.8×10⁻³?

Honest answer: that coefficient assumes relativistic charges; annihilation at rest in a metal involves charges moving at ~0.007c. The paper keeps the larger number as an upper estimate, and the conclusion (background ≪ signal) holds with either.

Q3. Kirchhoff's law is for thermal equilibrium. Your photons are emitted at specific depths.

Honest answer: correct; the paper calls it an approximation. In a 5 nm film the annihilation sites span most of the thickness, so it should be a fair estimate, but the paper recommends measuring the film's emissivity, and a depth-resolved calculation would be a good addition.

Q4. Table II depends on many assumed factors. How robust is it?

Honest answer: it scales simply: τ ∝ f_imp⁻², f_film⁻², η⁻¹ (noise and signal both carry η), bandwidth, area, and ∝ 1/f_esc. A factor-of-two worse η doubles τ. The 22Na conclusion (years) and facility conclusion (hours or less) survive factors of several.

Q5. If the photons existed, wouldn't they already have been seen in positron experiments?

Honest answer: unlikely. Positron experiments don't look for a few-meV far-infrared line with cold filters and bolometers, and the film's own thermal radiation is 10⁸ times brighter than the signal; it takes chopping and long integration to see it.

Key takeaways#

  • Geant4 supplies geometry only; signal and background are inputs, and the background is estimated analytically.
  • Background: 4×10⁻⁹ (slow charges) to 1.7×10⁻⁴ (upper) photons per annihilation per band, vs 2 for the signal; it doesn't move with T.
  • Escape 6.75% at 5 nm; implanted 90% and in-film 85% are assumptions to be measured.
  • Noise = film's own glow; τ ∝ T³; Table II: 30 min (77 K) / 3.7 h (150 K) at 4.5×10⁷ s⁻¹.
  • Positive result measures I via λT; null result limits Br within the stated assumptions.
