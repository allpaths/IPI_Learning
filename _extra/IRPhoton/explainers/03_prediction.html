Where this section fits#

Section II has one job: take Vopson's 2022 protocol paper (AIP Advances 12, 035311) and turn it into a number you could go and look for. It does this in four short steps, the same line of argument Vopson's paper uses:

  1. A bit has an energy (Landauer), and M/E/I says it therefore has an equivalent mass.
  2. Annihilation erases a particle completely, so the particle's information has to go somewhere.
  3. The energy balance says it leaves as two extra photons, and gives their energy and wavelength.
  4. The wavelength depends on temperature, and that dependence is what the experiment uses.

The section then lists, plainly, everything that had to be assumed to get there. Each heading below matches a subsection of the manuscript. The manuscript text is quoted in a grey box, followed by the plain-language unpacking.

1. Information content of the electron and positron#

Manuscript · II.A, paragraph 1Landauer showed that erasing one bit of information at temperature \(T\) dissipates at least \(k_b T \ln 2\) of energy [1]. This bound has been tested with a colloidal particle in an optical trap [5] and in a feedback trap [6], and with nanomagnetic memory bits [7]. The mass–energy–information (M/E/I) equivalence principle goes one step further. It proposes that a stored bit carries an equivalent mass [2], [4] …

Equation (1): the mass of a bit

\[ m_{bit} = \frac{k_b T \ln 2}{c^2} \qquad (1)\]
SymbolPlain meaningValue / unit
\(k_b\)Boltzmann's constant. It converts a temperature into a typical energy.1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K (exact)
\(T\)Temperature of the place where the bit lives (later: the metal target).kelvin (K)
\(\ln 2\)Natural log of 2 ≈ 0.693. It appears because one bit is a choice between 2 states.dimensionless
\(k_b T\ln 2\)The Landauer energy: the minimum heat released when one bit is erased.17.9 meV at 300 K
\(c\)Speed of light. Dividing an energy by \(c^2\) turns it into a mass (\(E=mc^2\) run backwards).2.998 × 10⁸ m/s

Step by step. Landauer says erasing a bit releases \(k_bT\ln2\). M/E/I says this energy was "stored" in the bit as mass all along, so \(m_{bit}c^2 = k_bT\ln2\). Divide both sides by \(c^2\) and you get Eq. (1). The algebra is trivial. The physics is all in the assumption that an erasure cost equals a stored rest energy. That is the same point the IPI_Learning Part 9 page makes ("a proposal, not a deduction").

Where 1.509 bits comes from

Vopson's 2021 paper (AIP Advances 11, 105317, the paper's ref. [3]) treats the matter of the universe as a bag of particles and asks a Shannon question: how many bits, on average, does it take to say which kind of particle you have just picked? Counting electrons, protons and neutrons gives 1.288 bits. Counting electrons, up quarks and down quarks instead gives 1.509 bits per particle. The positron was not part of that estimate; the 2021 paper explicitly leaves antiparticles out. Vopson 2022 simply assumes the positron carries the same amount as the electron, and the manuscript follows that assumption and says so (assumption 1).

Why it matters. The manuscript's line "about 2 × 10⁷ times smaller than its rest mass" explains why nobody can just weigh the information. The only way in is to erase it and catch the energy that comes out. That motivates the next subsection.

2. Erasure by annihilation#

Manuscript · II.BTo erase the information held by an elementary particle completely, the particle itself has to be removed from existence. Electron–positron annihilation does exactly this [4]. … Both particles are effectively at rest and at the target temperature \(T\), so their kinetic energies can be neglected [4].

In plain words. You can't reach into an electron and reset its bits. But when an electron meets its antiparticle, both vanish and only light is left, so whatever information they held is gone. That makes annihilation a natural erasure experiment.

Why "slow" and "at the target temperature". The positron is fired into a metal sheet. It loses its energy and settles to the metal's temperature within a few picoseconds, but typically lives 100–450 picoseconds before annihilating (Vopson 2022, §IV). So by the time it annihilates, it has the metal's temperature, and so does the electron it meets. That is why there is a single \(T\) in the equations, and why the experimenter controls it by setting the target temperature. Its kinetic energy (a few eV at most, thermal energies ~0.03 eV) is negligible next to 511 keV.

Equation (2): the energy balance (Vopson 2022, Eq. 10)

\[ m_{e^-}c^2 + m_{e^+}c^2 + I_{e^-}k_bT\ln 2 + I_{e^+}k_bT\ln 2 = \gamma + \gamma + \nu^{+} + \nu^{-} \qquad (2)\]
TermPlain meaning
\(m_{e^-}c^2 + m_{e^+}c^2\)Rest energies of the electron and positron, 511 keV each. Standard physics.
\(I_{e^-}k_bT\ln2\)The electron's information energy: its number of bits × the Landauer energy per bit.
\(I_{e^+}k_bT\ln2\)The same for the positron.
\(\gamma + \gamma\)The two ordinary 511 keV gamma photons that every annihilation textbook describes.
\(\nu^{+},\ \nu^{-}\)The two information energy photons (Vopson's name and notation). This is the new prediction.

Read it like a receipt. The left side is what goes in; the right side is what comes out. Without the information terms you recover the textbook result: two gammas. Add the information terms and, if energy is conserved, two more photons must carry them away.

NotationThe manuscript uses Vopson's own symbols \(\nu^{+},\nu^{-}\) and the name "information energy photons". A reader comparing with the 2022 paper sees the same symbols. One wrinkle: Vopson uses \(\nu\) both as the photon's name and (in \(h\nu\)) for its frequency. The manuscript keeps that, as he does.

3. Energy and wavelength of the photons#

Manuscript · II.C, paragraph 1Each particle's information is released as its own photon. Because \(I_{e^-} = I_{e^+} = I\), the two photons are identical, and each has energy …
\[ h\nu = I\,k_bT\ln2 \qquad (3) \qquad\qquad \lambda = \frac{hc}{I\,k_bT\ln 2} \qquad (4)\]

Step by step from (2) to (3) and (4):

  1. The gammas take the rest energies, so the information terms must go into \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\).
  2. One photon per particle: \(\nu^-\) carries \(I_{e^-}k_bT\ln2\) and \(\nu^+\) carries \(I_{e^+}k_bT\ln2\). They are equal because \(I_{e^-}=I_{e^+}\). Note that each photon carries the full amount for one particle; the energy is not split in half.
  3. A photon's energy is \(h\nu\), where \(h\) is Planck's constant and \(\nu\) is its frequency. That gives Eq. (3).
  4. Frequency and wavelength are linked by \(\lambda = c/\nu\). Substitute and you get Eq. (4), which is Vopson 2022's Eq. (11).

Worked example: 300 K, room temperature

Landauer energy per bit: \(k_bT\ln2 = (1.380649\times10^{-23})(300)(0.6931) = 2.871\times10^{-21}\) J = 17.92 meV.

× 1.509 bits = 4.332 × 10⁻²¹ J = 27.04 meV per photon.

Wavelength: \(\lambda = hc/E = (6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8}) / 4.332\times10^{-21}\) = 4.585 × 10⁻⁵ m = 45.9 µm.

For scale, that is 27 meV next to a 511 000 000 meV gamma: about 5 × 10⁻⁸ of the gamma's energy. Infrared at 46 µm is "far infrared", far longer than what thermal cameras use (8–14 µm).

Equation (5): the temperature handle

Manuscript · II.C, paragraph 2The useful feature of Eq. (4) is its dependence on temperature. Multiplying both sides by \(T\) gives a constant …
\[ \lambda\,T = \frac{hc}{I\,k_b\ln2} \approx 13.76\ \text{mm K} \qquad (5)\]

What it says. Wavelength × temperature is fixed, so if you halve the temperature the wavelength doubles. Going from 150 K to 77 K moves the predicted photons from 91.7 µm to 178.6 µm, a factor of 150/77 = 1.948. The experiment's idea is: measure at one temperature, change it, and check that the infrared signal moves by exactly the predicted ratio.

A nice property worth knowing: the ratio \(\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = T_2/T_1\) does not depend on \(I\) at all. Even if 1.509 bits turned out to be wrong, the predicted shift would be the same. Only the absolute position of the line depends on \(I\).

5. The assumptions, one by one#

Vopson 2022 is candid that its protocol "makes the assumption that the information content is conserved … via the production of two IR photons" and calls this "a strong assumption". The manuscript keeps that honesty and makes the list explicit. Here is why each one matters, and what happens to the experiment if it is wrong.

#Assumption (as in the manuscript)Why it mattersIf it's wrong…
1Information content: both particles carry 1.509 bits; the positron value is assumed.Sets where the line sits (45.9 µm at 300 K).The line sits somewhere else, but it still shifts as 1/T. A scan over wavelength would still find it.
2Every annihilation releases the photons.Sets how bright the signal is, and so how long the measurement takes (Section V).If only a fraction do, the signal is proportionally weaker and the measurement takes longer, quadratically so for a noise-limited detector.
3Two photons, one per particle, in opposite directions; no preferred orientation.Sets the energy per photon, and tells you where to put detectors (answer: everywhere).If one photon carried both shares, it would have twice the energy (22.9 µm at 300 K). Any preferred direction is unknown, so the design collects light from all around.
4Carrier: the energy leaves as infrared photons, not imprinted on the gammas.This is the whole premise of looking in the infrared.The experiment sees nothing even if M/E/I is right. Vopson 2022 says this itself in its Conclusions.
5Temperature: T is the metal target's temperature.It is the knob the experiment turns.Positrons that escape the surface (e.g. as positronium) may annihilate at another temperature. That is treated as a systematic in Section V.

The "literal-energy" reading

Manuscript · II.D, last paragraphFinally, this work tests what may be called the literal-energy reading of M/E/I … Other readings exist. In one, information mass is an equivalent representation of information in units of mass, a mathematical tool, rather than a literal inertial mass. …

Why this paragraph is there. "Information mass" can also be read as a unit conversion (information expressed in kilograms) rather than real inertial mass. If that is the right reading, nothing physical needs to fly out when a particle is annihilated, and the experiment wouldn't test it. The paragraph is honest about this: the experiment tests one specific, literal version of the idea. A null result would rule out that version (given assumptions 1–5), not M/E/I as a whole.

6. Questions a referee might ask#

Honest answers, including the uncomfortable ones. Click to expand.

Q1. Landauer's principle is about heat dumped into an environment. Why should erased information come out as photons with a definite energy?

Honest answer: it doesn't follow from Landauer. Landauer gives a minimum heat into a reservoir, not a specific quantum. The two discrete photons are part of Vopson's M/E/I model (Eq. 2), and that is exactly what the experiment tests. The manuscript says so by calling it the "literal-energy reading" and listing it as assumption 4. It is presented as the hypothesis under test, not as something derived.

Q2. Why two photons? Couldn't one extra photon conserve momentum, with the gammas recoiling slightly?

Honest answer: yes, in principle. With two gammas present, a tiny recoil can balance one extra soft photon (ordinary three-photon annihilation does this all the time). "Two photons" is Vopson's model (one per particle), which is why the paper writes "Following Ref. 4", lists it as an assumption, and now says explicitly that momentum conservation does not require two photons. If a single photon carried both shares, it would sit at half the wavelength (22.9 µm at 300 K) and would still shift as 1/T. An experiment that scans wavelength and collects from all directions is sensitive to both cases.

Q3. The target's own thermal glow also shifts with temperature (Wien's law). How is your signal different?

Honest answer: a good and important question. Thermal emission is a broad continuum whose peak obeys λpeakT ≈ 2.90 mm K. The predicted signal is a narrow line at λT ≈ 13.76 mm K, about 4.7× longer than the thermal peak. More importantly, it is produced only when annihilations happen. Switching the positron beam on and off (lock-in detection, Section III), synchronized with the 511 keV gammas, separates "light caused by annihilations" from "light the warm target always gives off". So the claim is not "nothing else moves with T"; it is "a line that appears only with the beam and moves with T". The thermal glow still sets the noise level; that is a Section V issue.

Q4. 1.509 bits is itself an unconfirmed estimate, and nobody estimated it for positrons. Isn't the prediction built on sand?

Honest answer: the number is an estimate, and the positron value is assumed (the manuscript says so). But the test does not hinge on it. The ratio of wavelengths at two temperatures, T2/T1, is independent of I. A measured line position would actually measure I. For what the 1.509 figure is and is not, see the bits-per-particle explainer, §6.

Q5. Which temperature: the positron's or the electron's? And what about positrons that leave the surface as positronium?

Honest answer: the positron thermalizes within picoseconds and lives 100–450 ps in a metal, so both particles share the target temperature (Vopson 2022, Eq. 9 discussion). For positronium annihilating in vacuum there is no obvious temperature. The manuscript flags this as assumption 5 and defers it to Section V as a systematic.

Q6. Why is each photon's energy the full I k_bT ln2, not half of it?

Honest answer: in Eq. (2) each particle contributes its own term, and each photon carries one particle's term (Vopson 2022: ν+ = ν− = ν, Eq. 11 has no factor ½). The total extra energy is 2·I·kbT ln2, shared as one I·kbT ln2 per photon.

Q7. If you see nothing, have you disproved M/E/I?

Honest answer: no, and the manuscript says so. A null result rules out the literal-energy reading under assumptions 1–5. It cannot exclude the "imprinted on the gammas" scenario or the "mathematical tool" reading. Saying this up front makes the paper more credible, not less.

Key takeaways

  • One line of argument: a bit has an energy → annihilation erases the particle → two extra photons → their wavelength is set by temperature.
  • Every number comes from one formula, λ = hc/(I kbT ln2), with I = 1.509 bits. The script reproduces Vopson 2022's values within his rounding.
  • The test is the shift, not the absolute wavelength. The ratio between two temperatures doesn't depend on I.
  • Five assumptions are stated as assumptions. The biggest one is that the energy leaves as infrared photons at all.
  • Scope is honest: this tests the literal-energy reading of M/E/I, not every possible reading.
