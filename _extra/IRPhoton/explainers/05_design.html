Where this section fits#

Section III said what to do. Section IV says with what: a positron source, a target film, a gamma-ray detector and a far-infrared detector. Each subsection explains one piece in plain terms and gives the one or two numbers that matter for the estimates in Section V.

⚠ Where the Geant4 numbers come fromFour things in this section come from the Geant4 Monte Carlo model: the stopping depth, the cavity's collected fraction 0.984, the gamma response and the three-photon monitor. The model was re-run with Geant4 11.4.2 (October 2026), with ortho-positronium switched on in a second set of runs and a third scan of the ortho-positronium fraction up to 0.675; the re-run agrees with the first runs (Geant4 11.3.2) within 2%. The stopping depth at 100 eV is below the energy range where Geant4's positron transport is reliable, so it is indicative only. The paper and Supplement S2 say so.

Paragraph by paragraph#

A. Slow-positron source#

Paper · IV.A, paragraph 1Positrons from a radioactive source such as \(^{22}\)Na are emitted with energies up to about 0.5 MeV. To obtain a slow beam, they are passed through a moderator … The fraction is typically about \(10^{-4}\). A \(^{22}\)Na source of 1 GBq emits about \(9\times10^8\) positrons per second, so it yields of order \(10^5\) slow positrons per second.

Radioactive sources make fast positrons. To get slow ones, you let them stop in a moderator (usually tungsten). Tungsten has a negative work function for positrons, meaning a positron that wanders back to the surface after stopping is pushed out with a few eV. Only about one in ten thousand does that. So even a strong source gives about 10⁵ slow positrons per second.

Paper · IV.A, paragraph 2Reactor- and accelerator-based facilities … At the reactor source NEPOMUC … the measured primary beam is \(9\times10^8\) positrons per second at 1 keV, and a remoderated beam of \(4.5\times10^7\) per second has been delivered at 20–200 eV with a diameter below 2 mm. … We use its rate as the reference case. NEPOMUC has been out of operation since the FRM II reactor shut down in 2020 and is awaiting its restart; the rates in Table II apply to any beam of the stated intensity.

Big facilities make positrons from gamma rays (pair production) and get far more. NEPOMUC at the Munich research reactor is the example used. Two beams matter: the primary beam (brighter, but at 1 keV, so it would have to be slowed down) and the remoderated beam (fainter, but already at the right energy). The paper uses the remoderated rate, 4.5×10⁷ s⁻¹, as its reference because it needs no assumptions about slowing the beam.

Status of the source. NEPOMUC takes its positrons from the FRM II reactor in Garching, and the reactor has been shut down since March 2020 while a replacement central channel is made. Public reports in 2026 put the replacement in 2027, with no firm restart date. That is why the paper says the beam "has been delivered" rather than "is available", and adds that Table II applies to any beam of the stated intensity. The rate column is the input; the facility names are examples. Check the status again just before submission.

Chopping (paragraph 3) is done electrically, by deflecting the beam or switching the target voltage, a few hertz to a few hundred hertz.

B. Target#

Paper · IV.B, paragraph 1The target is an aluminum film of thickness \(d = 5\) nm. Its thickness is set by two competing needs. Positrons must stop inside it, and the infrared photons generated inside must get out. … A Geant4 Monte Carlo model … gives a mean stopping depth of 1.4 nm and a 90th percentile of 1.7 nm. These values are indicative only …

The trade-off: thick enough to stop the positrons, thin enough to let the light out (metal films are mirrors in the far infrared; Section V.C quantifies this). At 100 eV the positrons stop within about 2 nm, so 5 nm gives a margin of about three. A positron then lives a few hundred picoseconds, long after it has cooled to the film temperature, which is what the prediction needs.

Paper · IV.B, paragraph 2The film must also hold its temperature while the beam deposits energy in it. Because the beam is chopped, any heating is chopped too … We therefore assume the film is deposited on a high-resistivity silicon substrate about 0.5 mm thick …

A subtle point. The beam heats the film a little every time it is on. That makes the film's glow flicker in step with the beam, which the lock-in would keep. A 5 nm film alone can't carry the heat away fast enough, so the design puts it on a silicon wafer clamped to the cold stage. The Supplement (S6) finds the flicker is then about three times below the tolerable level at 150 K (it was six times before the positronium fraction was raised; the margin shrinks with the signal). The silicon is high-resistivity silicon: it conducts heat well but has few free electrons, so it absorbs little far-infrared light and barely affects how light escapes the film (S3). An earlier line in the conclusions said "conducting substrate"; it now says high-resistivity silicon too.

Paragraph 3: a 1% error in temperature shifts the predicted wavelength by 1%, small compared with the 10% band.

C. Gamma-ray detection#

Paper · IV.CA shell of scintillation detectors surrounds the target … Cerium bromide (CeBr\(_3\)) detectors combine good energy resolution with fast timing … The gamma-ray count measures the annihilation rate \(R\) … It can also monitor positronium. … The spin-1 state (ortho-positronium) decays to three gamma photons …

The gamma shell counts annihilations. That gives \(R\), and the ratio of three-photon to two-photon events tells you how many positrons formed positronium, a key systematic (Section V).

Paper · IV.C (monitor)Both decay modes are available in recent versions of Geant4 [21]. In our model of the shell (Supplementary Material, Sec. S2), nine in ten three-photon decays leave no photon in the 511 keV photopeak. The ratio of counts just below the photopeak to counts in it therefore rises with the fraction of stopped positrons that decay as ortho-positronium: from 0.035 with none, to 0.42 at 0.36 (three quarters of a positronium fraction of 0.48), and to 1.19 at 0.675. The rise is not linear … Real positronium flies off, and about half of it reaches the cavity wall. If it is quenched there into two 511 keV photons, the model reads a fraction of 0.13–0.19 instead of 0.36. … The monitor must therefore be calibrated in place.

Ref. [21] is Allison et al., "Positron annihilation into two or three gammas in Geant4" (Radiation Physics and Chemistry 251, 114505, 2027), written by the Geant4 developers. Since Geant4 11.3 the toolkit can make ortho-positronium decay into three photons, but only if the user says what fraction of positrons form it in each material. For the re-run we switched it on in the film only, with three fractions: 0, 0.1125 and 0.15. (0.1125 is three quarters of 15%: of all positronium, three quarters is the ortho kind that decays to three photons.)

The monitor works by looking just below the photopeak. A 511 keV photon that is fully absorbed lands in the peak; the three photons of an ortho-positronium decay share 1022 keV unevenly, so they mostly land lower down. The ratio \(R\) of counts in a "valley" window (350 to 490 keV) to counts in the peak therefore grows with the positronium fraction. The model gives \(R = 0.035\) with none, 0.131 at 0.1125, 0.28 at 0.25, 0.42 at 0.36, 0.68 at 0.5 and 1.19 at 0.675. The curve bends upward: the valley count goes up and the peak count goes down as the fraction grows, and a ratio of a rising number to a falling one is not a straight line. Each count on its own is a straight line in the fraction, so the fraction can still be read off the ratio with a simple formula (Supplement S2). The old "slope of 0.87" was measured only up to 0.15 and is right only near zero; at 0.36 it would have predicted 0.35 instead of 0.42. Even with no positronium \(R\) is not zero, because some 511 keV photons Compton-scatter and leave only part of their energy. That zero point changes with the geometry (0.028 for a bare film, 0.035 on silicon), which is why the paper says the monitor must be calibrated in place.

D. Far-infrared detection#

Paper · IV.D, paragraphs 1–2The predicted photons have no known preferred direction … so the film sits inside a closed, reflective collecting cavity … In the Geant4 geometry the cavity collects 98.4% … The detector is a bolometer … A transition-edge sensor (TES) … Far-infrared TES bolometers with NEP ≈ 3×10⁻¹⁹ W/√Hz have been demonstrated. … the noise in this experiment is set instead by the film's own thermal emission …

Since nobody knows which way the photons go, collect them all: a mirror-lined cavity around the film funnels light into a light pipe, through a cold filter for one band, onto a bolometer. A bolometer is a thermometer for light: it absorbs radiation and measures the tiny warming. A TES reads that warming from a superconducting film sitting right at its transition. Modern ones are so quiet that they are not the limit here; the film's own glow is.

Paper · IV.D, paragraph 3The cavity walls are held at 4–10 K. Their own emission in the signal bands is then at most about 1% of the film's; at 20 K it would add more than half again to the thermal power in the 77 K band.

The walls must be colder than the film, or they glow in the signal band too. The cell below and Supplement S5 give the numbers. A cold wall has a bonus: positrons that bounce off the film and annihilate on the wall would (if M/E/I holds) emit at the wall's wavelength, 1.4 mm or longer, far from both bands.

Questions a referee might ask#

Q1. Vopson proposed a tabletop ²²Na experiment. Why move to a reactor facility?

Honest answer: the rate. With ~10⁵ slow positrons per second, Table II gives 31 years at 77 K. The facility beam brings that to 1.4 hours. The tabletop version is still useful for the bench tests in Section VI, just not for the detection.

Q2. Your stopping depth comes from Geant4 below its validity range. Can the film thickness be trusted?

Honest answer: only roughly, which is why the film is 5 nm, about three times the estimated range, and why the paper flags it. If positrons go deeper than expected, they still annihilate in the film (they diffuse ~150 nm before annihilating anyway); the main risk is a thicker film being needed, which lowers escape roughly as 1/d.

Q3. Doesn't the silicon substrate change the physics: positrons could annihilate in silicon?

Honest answer: yes, some may cross the interface; the paper says this is not modeled. They would annihilate at the same temperature (the substrate is in contact with the film), so the wavelength is unchanged; the escape of light from inside silicon would differ. A measurement of the annihilation-site fractions on the real sample would settle it.

Q4. Why trust that the detector isn't the limit?

Honest answer: the film's photon noise is ~10⁻¹⁴ W/√Hz, and the script uses a detector NEP of 10⁻¹⁷, about thirty times worse than demonstrated devices (3×10⁻¹⁹, Williams 2020, and again in 2026 by Sonka et al., refs. [24, 25]), and it still contributes nothing. The real question is optical efficiency (η = 0.5, assumed), not detector noise.

Q5. NEPOMUC has been shut down since 2020. Is the headline 1.4 hours real?

Honest answer: the number is real for a beam of 4.5×10⁷ s⁻¹ at about 100 eV. Whether such a beam can be had on a given date is a separate question, and the paper now says so. NEPOMUC measured that rate before the shutdown and expects to restart with the reactor. Table II gives the time for any rate, so another facility can read off its own number (10⁷ s⁻¹ gives 1.1 days at 77 K). Linac-based sources are the alternative. The design does not depend on one facility.

Q6. Can the gamma shell really see three-photon decays?

Honest answer: in the model, yes, and clearly. With 36% of stopped positrons decaying as ortho-positronium (the nominal clean-surface case), 34% of all annihilations give three photons, and in nine of ten of those no photon falls in the 511 keV photopeak. The valley-to-peak ratio goes from 0.035 to 0.42, and its statistical error after 2×10⁵ annihilations is about 0.003, so a change of one percent in the fraction would show. Two things are approximate. Geant4 decays the positronium where the positron stopped, inside the film; real positronium leaves the surface and flies several centimetres, and about half of it reaches the cavity wall. A test run that moved the decays into vacuum changed the ratio by less than 2% if the positronium still decays into three photons at the wall, but if the wall quenches it into two 511 keV photons the monitor reads only 0.13–0.19 instead of 0.36. And the ratio's zero point depends on the geometry. Both mean the real monitor has to be calibrated on the apparatus. Heating the film is not a good way to do that on a 5 nm film on silicon; a controlled oxygen dose, which changes the fraction a lot at fixed temperature, is a better candidate.

Key takeaways#

  • A facility slow-positron beam (reference: NEPOMUC remoderated, 4.5×10⁷ s⁻¹ at 20–200 eV) replaces the ²²Na source.
  • Target: 5 nm Al on silicon, at 77 K and 150 K; thin for escape, on a substrate for heat.
  • Gamma shell: counts \(R\), monitors positronium (simulated: the valley-to-peak ratio rises from 0.035 to 0.42 as the ortho-positronium fraction goes from 0 to 0.36, curving upward; must be calibrated in place).
  • NEPOMUC is the reference rate, but it has been offline since 2020; Table II applies to any beam of that intensity.
  • Far-IR: 4π cavity at 4–10 K, cold 10% filters, TES bolometer; the film's glow, not the detector, sets the noise.
  • Geant4 inputs (depth, collection, gamma response) come from a re-run with Geant4 11.4.2; the 100 eV depth is flagged as indicative.
