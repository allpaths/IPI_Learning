Where 1.509 bits per particle comes from

A worked companion to Vopson, "Estimation of the information contained in the visible matter of the universe," AIP Advances 11, 105317 (2021): the Shannon entropy behind the number Section II relies on, reproduced step by step, with an honest look at what it does and does not mean.

Where this fits#

Section II predicts that an annihilating electron–positron pair emits two information energy photons with wavelength \(\lambda = hc/(I\,k_bT\ln2)\). Everything about where that line sits depends on one input: \(I\), the number of bits each particle is supposed to hold. The manuscript takes \(I = 1.509\) bits and cites it directly to Vopson 2021, ref. [3]:

Manuscript · II.A, paragraph 2Using Shannon's information theory, an earlier estimate found that each stable elementary particle in the visible matter of the universe stores about \(I = 1.509\) bits of information about itself [3]. This figure is the average information content per particle over the relative abundances of up quarks, down quarks and electrons [3]. That estimate excluded antiparticles. Following [4], we assume that the positron carries the same amount as the electron …

This page unpacks that sentence. It does four things:

  1. explains the formula, Shannon entropy;
  2. shows which particles were counted and why;
  3. reproduces 1.509 with a short script;
  4. lists what a referee could fairly question.

It also briefly covers the paper's second result, about 6 × 1080 bits in the visible universe, which Section II does not use.

1. Shannon entropy in one page#

Shannon (1948) asked how much information you gain when you learn the outcome of a random event. His answer is that a rare outcome tells you more than a common one. One outcome of probability \(p\) carries \(-\log_2 p\) bits; this is called its surprisal. Averaged over all the possible outcomes, that gives the Shannon entropy, which is Vopson 2021's Eq. (4) with base 2:

\[ H(X) = -\sum_{j=1}^{n} p_j \log_2 p_j \quad \text{bits} \]
SymbolPlain meaning
\(X\)The set of possible outcomes. Here these are the kinds of particle you might pick out of the universe.
\(p_j\)The probability of outcome \(j\), here the fraction of all particles that are of kind \(j\). The probabilities add up to 1.
\(-\log_2 p_j\)The surprisal of that outcome: how many yes/no questions it takes to pin it down.
\(H\)The average surprisal: the average number of bits needed to say which kind of particle you picked.

Three anchor cases.

  • A fair coin has two equally likely outcomes, so \(H = 1\) bit. That is the definition of a bit.
  • Three equally likely kinds give \(H = \log_2 3 = 1.585\) bits, the maximum possible for three kinds.
  • Any lopsided mix gives less, because the common outcome is unsurprising.
import math def H(probs): # Shannon entropy in bits, Vopson 2021 Eq. (4) with b = 2 return -sum(p*math.log2(p) for p in probs) print("fair coin {1/2, 1/2} :", H([0.5, 0.5]), "bit") print("three equal types :", round(H([1/3]*3), 4), "bits = log2(3)") print("biased coin {0.9, 0.1} :", round(H([0.9, 0.1]), 4), "bits")
fair coin {1/2, 1/2} : 1.0 bit three equal types : 1.585 bits = log2(3) biased coin {0.9, 0.1} : 0.469 bits

The key move in Vopson 2021 is to treat "observing a particle" as the random event. The probabilities are the cosmic abundances of each particle type. The entropy \(H\) is then read as "the information each particle stores about itself". Section 6 comes back to whether that reading is justified.

2. Which particles are counted, and why#

The Standard Model has 30 particles and antiparticles. Vopson 2021 keeps only the ones that, in its argument, make up today's visible matter and can store information:

GroupCounted?Reason given in Vopson 2021
Force carriers (photon, gluon, W, Z) and the Higgs✘Postulated to transfer information "via waveform", not store it. Only "stable" particles with "a non-zero rest mass" are taken to store information (Sec. I).
Unstable particles (muon, tau, heavy quarks, …)✘Their lifetimes are so short that their share of the observable universe is negligible.
Antiparticles (including the positron)✘Excluded explicitly: "Excluding the bosons, the anti-particles and all the unstable elementary particles…" (Sec. II).
Neutrinos✘"We also ignored … all neutrinos" (Sec. IV).
Electron✔Stable, massive, and present in every atom.
Proton and neutron (step 1)✔Make up every nucleus. Counted first as if they were elementary.
Up and down quarks (step 2)✔Replace the proton (2 up + 1 down) and the neutron (1 up + 2 down), because those two "are in fact not elementary particles".

So the calculation is done twice:

  • first over the set {p+, e−, n0}, which gives 1.288 bits;
  • then over {up quark, down quark, e−}, which gives 1.509 bits.

Section II uses the second number.

3. The probabilities#

Where the counts come from.

  • Vopson starts from the abundance of the chemical elements by weight: about 75% hydrogen, 23% helium and 2% heavier elements. His source is Mathematica's ElementData, ref. 36 of the paper.
  • He converts this to abundance by number of atoms: 92.68% hydrogen and 7.15% helium.
  • He then adds up the electrons, protons and neutrons in every element.

The full element table is in the paper's supplementary material, which is not in our files, so I take his totals as printed. A cross-check with hydrogen and helium alone gives 106.98 electrons and 14.3 neutrons per 100 atoms. The heavy elements, only 0.17% of atoms, supply the remaining 1.3 of each, which is plausible.

The paper's 216.55 and 31.21 are 0.01 above 2 × 108.27 = 216.54 and 2 × 15.6 = 31.20. This is probably rounding in the unpublished supplement, and it makes no difference to the result.

The universe is mostly hydrogen, so the mix is close to "one proton, one electron": 2 up : 1 down : 1 electron. That alone gives \(H = 1.5\) bits exactly, which is why the answer lands so close to 1.5.

4. Reproducing 1.509, step by step#

\[ H_{pen} = -\left(P_{p^+}\log_2 P_{p^+} + P_{e^-}\log_2 P_{e^-} + P_{n^0}\log_2 P_{n^0}\right) \qquad \text{(Vopson 2021, Eq. 6)} \] \[ H_{uqdqe} = -\left(P_{uq}\log_2 P_{uq} + P_{dq}\log_2 P_{dq} + P_{e^-}\log_2 P_{e^-}\right) \qquad \text{(Eq. 7)} \]
import math H = lambda ps: -sum(p*math.log2(p) for p in ps) # Step 1: electrons, protons, neutrons per 100 atoms (Vopson 2021, Sec. II) n_e, n_p, n_n = 108.27, 108.27, 15.6 tot = n_e + n_p + n_n P_pen = [n_p/tot, n_e/tot, n_n/tot] print(f"step 1: total = {tot:.2f} P = {[round(p,3) for p in P_pen]}") print(f" H_pen = {H(P_pen):.4f} bits (paper: 1.288)") # Step 2: replace each proton by 2 up + 1 down quark, each neutron by 1 up + 2 down uq = 216.55 + 15.6 # up quarks in protons + in neutrons (paper: 232.15) dq = 108.27 + 31.21 # down quarks in protons + in neutrons (paper: 139.48) N = uq + dq + n_e # + electrons (paper: 479.9) P = [uq/N, dq/N, n_e/N] print(f"step 2: uq = {uq:.2f} dq = {dq:.2f} N = {N:.2f}") print(f" P = {[round(p,4) for p in P]} (paper: 0.483, 0.29, 0.225)") print(f" H_uqdqe, full precision = {H(P):.4f} bits") print(f" H_uqdqe, paper's printed P's = {H([0.483, 0.29, 0.225]):.4f} bits -> {H([0.483, 0.29, 0.225]):.3f}") print("match 1.509:", round(H([0.483, 0.29, 0.225]), 3) == 1.509)
step 1: total = 232.14 P = [0.466, 0.466, 0.067] H_pen = 1.2882 bits (paper: 1.288) step 2: uq = 232.15 dq = 139.48 N = 479.90 P = [0.4837, 0.2906, 0.2256] (paper: 0.483, 0.29, 0.225) H_uqdqe, full precision = 1.5096 bits H_uqdqe, paper's printed P's = 1.5092 bits -> 1.509 match 1.509: True

Result. 1.288 and 1.509 are both reproduced.

A rounding detail worth knowingUsing the paper's printed probabilities {0.483, 0.29, 0.225} gives 1.5092, i.e. 1.509 exactly. Carrying the counts at full precision gives 1.5096, which would round to 1.510. The difference is 0.03%. It moves λT from 13.756 to 13.750 mm K, which is invisible in Table 1 of Section II. Keep 1.509 because that is the published value, but don't claim four significant figures of physics for it.

Worked by hand for step 2, using the printed probabilities:

  • −0.483 log₂0.483 = 0.507
  • −0.29 log₂0.29 = 0.518
  • −0.225 log₂0.225 = 0.484

The sum is 1.509 bits.

5. The second result: about 6 × 1080 bits in the visible universe#

The paper then multiplies bits per particle by the number of particles. That number of particles is a modern version of the Eddington number. Eddington (1938) put the count of protons at 136 × 2256 ≈ 1.57 × 1079. The chain of steps is:

  1. Critical density. \(\rho_c = 3H^2/8\pi G\), with Hubble parameter \(H = 2.2\times10^{-18}\) s−1 (Eq. 8).
  2. Mass of ordinary matter. The baryon fraction is \(\Omega_b = 0.0485\). The observable universe is a sphere of radius \(L_u = 4.4\times10^{26}\) m (46.5 billion light-years). Together these give \(M_b = \Omega_b\rho_c \cdot \tfrac43\pi L_u^3 = H^2\Omega_b L_u^3/2G\) (Eq. 9).
  3. Average particle mass. The electron/proton/neutron mix gives \(m_{b,\text{eff}} = 0.466\,m_e + 0.466\,m_p + 0.067\,m_n\) (Eq. 10). Then \(N_b = M_b/m_{b,\text{eff}}\) (Eq. 11).
  4. Count elementary particles. Each nucleon is three quarks, so \(N_{tot} = N_b\,[P_{e^-} + 3(P_{p^+}+P_{n^0})] \approx 4\times10^{80}\) (Eq. 13).
  5. Bits. \(N_{bits} = N_{tot}\times 1.509 = 6.036\times10^{80}\) bits.
import math Hub, G, Om_b, L_u = 2.2e-18, 6.674e-11, 0.0485, 44e25 # s^-1, SI, -, m (Vopson 2021, Sec. III) m_e, m_p, m_n = 9.109e-31, 1.672e-27, 1.675e-27 # kg P_e, P_p, P_n = 0.466, 0.466, 0.067 rho_c = 3*Hub**2/(8*math.pi*G) # Eq. (8) V_u = 4/3*math.pi*L_u**3 M_b = Hub**2*Om_b*L_u**3/(2*G) # Eq. (9) = Om_b * rho_c * V_u m_eff = P_e*m_e + P_p*m_p + P_n*m_n # Eq. (10) N_b = M_b/m_eff # Eq. (11) N_tot = N_b*(P_e + 3*(P_p + P_n)) # Eq. (13): electrons + 3 quarks per nucleon print(f"rho_c = {rho_c:.3e} kg/m^3 (paper prints 9.6e-27)") print(f"V_u = {V_u:.3e} m^3 (paper: 4e80)") print(f"N_b = {N_b:.3e} (paper: 1.93e80)") print(f"N_tot = {N_tot:.3e} (paper: 4e80)") print(f"N_bits = N_tot x 1.509 = {N_tot*1.509:.2e} (paper: 4e80 x 1.509 = {4e80*1.509:.3e})") print(f"Eddington's own number 136 x 2^256 = {136*2**256:.3e} protons")
rho_c = 8.656e-27 kg/m^3 (paper prints 9.6e-27) V_u = 3.568e+80 m^3 (paper: 4e80) N_b = 1.680e+80 (paper: 1.93e80) N_tot = 3.469e+80 (paper: 4e80) N_bits = N_tot x 1.509 = 5.23e+80 (paper: 4e80 x 1.509 = 6.036e+80) Eddington's own number 136 x 2^256 = 1.575e+79 protons

What checks and what doesn't:

  • The final step is exact: 4 × 1080 × 1.509 = 6.036 × 1080.
  • The intermediate numbers are not mutually consistent at the 10–15% level:
    • With the stated \(H\) and \(G\), \(\rho_c\) comes out as 8.66 × 10−27 kg m−3, not the printed 9.6 × 10−27. The printed value corresponds to \(H \approx 2.32\times10^{-18}\) s−1.
    • Even with the printed \(\rho_c\), \(N_b\) is 1.86 × 1080, not 1.93 × 1080.
    • \(N_{tot}\) is rounded up to 4 × 1080 before the final multiplication.
  • Carried through without rounding, the inputs give 5.2–5.8 × 1080 bits.

The headline "~6 × 1080" survives as an order of magnitude, and Section II does not depend on it. If you ever quote it, say "of order 1081 bits" or "~6 × 1080 (Vopson 2021)", and don't present it as a three-figure number.

Questions a referee might ask

Here are honest answers, including the uncomfortable ones. Click a question to expand it.

Q1. Why should the positron carry 1.509 bits when the derivation is about the populations of matter particles?

Honest answer: it isn't derived; it is assumed. Vopson 2021 explicitly leaves out antiparticles, and positrons are essentially absent from the population being averaged. Vopson 2022 says so: "Although the estimation accounted only for stable elementary particles excluding anti-particles, we can assume that the information content of the positron is equal to that of the electron."

The natural argument for the assumption is symmetry. A positron has the same mass and spin as an electron and the opposite charge, so it is described by the same three degrees of freedom that Vopson 2021 calls the particle's "DNA".

That argument has a weak point. The 1.509 figure is not a count of degrees of freedom. It is an average over how common each particle type is. If positrons were added to the population at their real cosmic abundance, their surprisal would be enormous but their weight in the average negligible. The average would barely change, and it would say nothing about what a single positron holds.

The manuscript handles this correctly: it lists the positron value as assumption 1 and points out that the predicted shift λ(T₁)/λ(T₂) = T₂/T₁ does not depend on \(I\).

Q2. Is 1.509 bits a property of a particle, or of a population?

Honest answer: mathematically, of a population. Shannon entropy is the average surprisal of a draw from a distribution. Particle by particle the surprisals differ:

  • up quark: 1.05 bits;
  • down quark: 1.78 bits;
  • electron: 2.15 bits.

If an electron "stored" its own surprisal, Section II would use \(I = 2.15\) and predict 32.2 µm at 300 K instead of 45.9 µm. Vopson 2021 interprets the average as the most efficient compression per particle ("the most effective way that information can be compressed into a p+, e−, or n0"). That is a reasonable reading for encoding a catalogue of particles, but it is an interpretation, not a theorem.

Expect this question. The defensible reply is that the experiment treats \(I\) as a parameter, and a detected line would measure it.

Q3. The positrons annihilate in an aluminium film, not in "the universe". Shouldn't the population be the target's?

Honest answer: the method gives no rule for choosing the population. Applying the same recipe to the particles of the target gives different values:

  • aluminium-27: 1.441 bits (λ = 48.0 µm at 300 K);
  • natural copper: 1.430 bits;
  • pure hydrogen: 1.500 bits.

Vopson 2022 itself applies the universal 1.509 to a copper sample. The differences are 5% or less, so this is a small effect on where the line sits and no effect on how it shifts with temperature. It is still a sign that the number is a modelling choice rather than a fixed constant. These target values are my calculations with the paper's recipe; they are not in either paper.

Q4. Why stop at quarks? And why exclude photons and neutrinos, which vastly outnumber baryons?

Honest answer: both are choices made in the paper.

  • Level of description. Moving from nucleons to quarks changed the answer from 1.288 to 1.509 bits. A finer description (quark colour, spin orientation, sea quarks) or a coarser one (atoms) would change it again. The paper gives no principle that fixes the level.
  • Photons and neutrinos. These are left out by the postulate that only stable, massive matter particles store information. Including the cosmic microwave background photons, roughly 109 per baryon, would completely change the distribution.

Section II should not try to defend these choices. It should cite them as the origin of the estimate and treat \(I\) as an input.

Q5. Vopson 2022 calls 1.509 "the maximum amount of information that could be stored per elementary particle". Is it a maximum?

Honest answer: no. For three particle types the maximum Shannon entropy is log₂3 = 1.585 bits, reached when all three are equally common. Vopson 2021 says this itself. The value 1.509 is the entropy of the actual mix, which is below the maximum. The manuscript does not repeat the word "maximum", and it shouldn't.

Q6. Does any of this affect the test in Section II?

Honest answer: it affects where the line sits, not how it moves. Plausible alternatives to 1.509 range from about 1.43 bits (local target) to 2.15 bits (electron surprisal). At 300 K that range spans about 32–48 µm. The ratio λ(77 K)/λ(150 K) = 1.948 holds for any \(I\).

This is why the apparatus should cover a band of wavelengths around 45.9 µm (scaled to each temperature) rather than a single narrow filter. It is also why a detection would be a measurement of \(I\), not merely a confirmation of it.

Key takeaways

  • 1.509 is a Shannon entropy. It is the average number of bits needed to say whether a particle drawn from visible matter is an up quark, a down quark or an electron.
  • It reproduces exactly from the paper's printed probabilities. Full precision gives 1.5096, which makes no practical difference.
  • It reflects a hydrogen-dominated universe. Pure hydrogen alone gives 1.5.
  • The positron value is an assumption. The 2021 estimate excluded antiparticles, and Vopson 2022 assumes equality with the electron.
  • The test in Section II does not hinge on it. The wavelength ratio between two temperatures is independent of \(I\), and a detection would measure \(I\).

Glossary

Shannon entropyThe average information, in bits, gained by learning the outcome of a random draw.
surprisalThe information in one specific outcome: −log₂ p.
up / down quarkThe constituents of protons (up, up, down) and neutrons (up, down, down).
abundance by weight vs by numberHelium is 23% of ordinary matter by mass but only about 7% of the atoms, because each helium atom is about 4 times heavier than a hydrogen atom.
