Where this section fits#

Section II gave a number to look for: a line at \(\lambda = 13.76\ \text{mm K}/T\). Section III says how to look for it, in seven steps taken from Vopson's 2022 paper, and explains why comparing two temperatures is what makes the test work. The apparatus itself comes in Section IV.

Paragraph by paragraph#

The seven steps#

Paper · III, list1. Produce a beam of slow positrons … 2. Switch the beam on and off at a fixed frequency ("chopping") … 3. Implant the positrons into a thin metal film held at a known temperature … 4. Detect the 511 keV gamma photons and the far-infrared emission, and recover the part of each signal that is synchronous with the chopping. A lock-in amplifier does this … 5. Measure … in a narrow wavelength band centered on \(\lambda_1\) … 6. Change the film temperature to \(T_2\) … 7. Measure again, in the band at \(\lambda_1\) and in a second band at \(\lambda_2 = \lambda_1T_1/T_2\).

Read the list as three ideas. Steps 1–3 make annihilations happen at a known temperature. Step 4 tags everything the beam causes by switching the beam on and off: a lock-in amplifier keeps only the part of the detector signal that rises and falls in step with the switching, and throws away everything steady. Steps 5–7 are the actual test: look in the band where the line should be, change the temperature, and check that the line has moved to where it should now be.

Why two temperatures#

Paper · III, paragraph after Fig. 2Three kinds of signal could appear … Thermal radiation from the film and its surroundings is present whether or not the beam is on, so the lock-in removes it on average; what remains is its noise. Radiation that the beam causes but that is not tied to \(T\) … passes the lock-in but stays where it is when the temperature changes. The predicted photons pass the lock-in and move. … A signal that does not move is not the predicted one.

This is the heart of the protocol. Sort every possible signal by two questions: does it follow the beam? and does it move with T?

Follows the beam (passes lock-in)Moves with \(T\) as \(1/T\)
Thermal glow of film and wallsno (only adds noise)its spectrum changes, but it is removed on average
QED soft photons from any annihilationyesno
Beam heating of the filmyesbroadband, also shows in a control band
Predicted information energy photonsyesyes, as a narrow line

Only the predicted signal gets "yes" in both columns and stays narrow. That is why the paper calls the temperature shift the signature.

Paragraph 3: why 77 K and 150 K#

Paper · III, last paragraphWe take \(T_1 = 77\) K and \(T_2 = 150\) K. The wavelength changes by a factor of 1.95 … so bands of 10% width are well separated. Both are easy to reach with liquid nitrogen and a heater. On a clean aluminum surface the fraction of positrons that leave as positronium is the same at both, because heat releases extra positronium only above room temperature [Mills & Pfeiffer 1985]. … On aluminum with a fraction of a monolayer of oxygen, however, thermally released positronium already grows between about 100 and 230 K [Mills et al. 1991], so the surface must be kept clean, or the fraction measured at both temperatures.

Three reasons: the bands don't overlap; liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K, so the cold point is nearly free; and on a clean surface the positronium loss is the same at both points, so the only thing that changes between the two measurements is the predicted wavelength (and the photon energy). The catch is the word clean. A little oxygen on the surface makes the positronium loss change between 77 K and 150 K. That is why the paper asks for a clean film, or for the loss to be measured at both temperatures.

Questions a referee might ask#

Q1. The film's thermal glow also changes with temperature. Couldn't that fake a moving signal?

Honest answer: not through the lock-in. The thermal glow is there with the beam on or off, so it averages away; it only sets the noise. What could leak through is beam heating, which is chopped. That is broadband, so it also appears in the control band; the predicted line does not. The paper also sizes the substrate to keep heating below the signal (Supplement S6).

Q2. Why not just look at one temperature and see if there's a line?

Honest answer: a beam-correlated line at one temperature could be many things (a detector or filter artifact, a QED feature, heating). Requiring it to move to the predicted place when only \(T\) changes is much harder to fake. One temperature is a search; two is a test.

Q3. Why 10% bands, when narrower bands would cut noise?

Honest answer: the 1.509-bit value is an estimate. A 10% band keeps the line inside even if \(I\) is off by a few percent. The paper says narrower bands or a wavelength scan are the natural next step once a signal is seen, and that a 1% band would be ten times faster.

Q4. Isn't the gamma count unnecessary if you chop the beam anyway?

Honest answer: the chopper gives the timing, but the gamma count gives the rate \(R\) actually annihilating, which the expected signal is proportional to, and the three-gamma rate monitors positronium. Both are needed to interpret the intensity.

Key takeaways#

  • Seven steps: slow beam, chop, implant, lock-in, measure at \(T_1\), change \(T\), measure at \(T_2\).
  • The lock-in keeps what the beam causes; the temperature change separates the prediction from everything else the beam causes.
  • 77 K and 150 K give well-separated bands, are easy to reach, and keep positronium constant on a clean surface.
